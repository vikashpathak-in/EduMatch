Teach a New Skill with Worked Examples: A Four-Step Lesson Plan for Tutors

Published 27 November 2026 · 6 min read

When a learner is new to a topic, asking them to solve a difficult question immediately can leave them guessing at the steps. A worked example shows the process from start to finish. A tutor can then reduce the help gradually until the learner can solve a similar problem independently.

A worked example is more than a displayed answer. It explains the steps and the reason for each step. The tutor should make the thinking visible, then ask the learner to explain and apply the method.

Step 1: Model one complete example

Choose a clear problem that represents the skill being taught. Work through it slowly and explain why each step is needed. Avoid skipping mental calculations that a beginner may not know how to perform.

Example: Find 15% of 240. First find 10%: 240 ÷ 10 = 24. Then find 5%, which is half of 10%: 24 ÷ 2 = 12. Add the parts: 24 + 12 = 36. So, 15% of 240 is 36.

Step 2: Ask the learner to explain the method

Pause after each step and ask questions such as “Why did we find 10% first?” or “How do we know that 5% is half of 10%?” The goal is to check whether the learner understands the relationship, not just whether they can repeat the numbers.

If the learner cannot explain a step, revisit that part with a simpler example. Correct the misunderstanding before adding more difficulty.

Step 3: Fade the support

Give a similar question with some steps already shown and ask the learner to complete the missing parts.

Guided example: Find 12% of 150. The first step is shown: 10% of 150 is 15. Ask the learner to find 2% and then add the two parts. The learner should reach 15 + 3 = 18.

On the next question, remove more of the prompts. This gradual reduction helps the learner take responsibility for the process.

Step 4: Give an independent question with a new set of numbers

Ask the learner to solve 15% of 320 without a worked solution. The answer is 48. After the attempt, ask the learner to explain the method and check the result. A new set of numbers helps test whether the learner understands the method rather than memorising the earlier answer.

What tutors should look for

  • Can the learner explain why each step is used?
  • Can they complete a partly worked example without copying blindly?
  • Can they solve a new question independently?
  • Can they spot and correct an error in a worked solution?
  • Do they need another example, a simpler explanation or a different representation?

When to reduce the examples

Worked examples are especially useful when a learner is beginning a new or complex task. As the learner becomes more confident, move towards independent practice. Continuing to show every step when the learner is already proficient may provide too much support and too little opportunity to practise.

Use more than one example and vary the numbers or context. Ask the learner to compare the examples and identify the principle that stays the same. This makes it easier to transfer the method to a new question.

For parents and tutors

Teach the process, not just the answer. Model one example, ask the learner to explain it, remove support gradually and finish with independent practice.
