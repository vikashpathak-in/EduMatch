Five-Minute Exit Tickets: A Tutor's Guide to Checking Understanding

Published 28 November 2026 · 6 min read

A learner may follow an explanation during a lesson but still struggle to use the idea independently. A short exit ticket gives the tutor a quick sample of what the learner can recall, apply and explain before the session ends.

An exit ticket is not a full test. It is a small check that helps the tutor decide what to revisit, what to practise next and where the learner may need a different explanation.

1. Start with one clear learning goal

Choose one specific outcome for the session. For example: “The learner can add two fractions with different denominators and explain why a common denominator is needed.” A focused goal makes it easier to write useful questions and interpret the answers.

2. Ask three short questions

Use one prompt for recall, one for application and one for reflection. Keep the questions short enough for the learner to complete independently.

  1. Recall: “In your own words, what does a common denominator help us do?”
  2. Apply: “Solve ⅔ + ⅙. Show one step.”
  3. Reflect: “Which part still feels unclear, if any?”

For the second question, a correct solution is 4/6 + 1/6 = 5/6. The written step helps the tutor see whether the learner found an equivalent fraction rather than adding the denominators directly.

3. Give every learner time to respond

Ask the learner to write an answer before discussing it. In a one-to-one session, this can be a small sheet of paper or a shared document. In a group, use mini-whiteboards, response cards or a short form so that every learner commits to an answer.

Avoid relying only on the first volunteer. One confident answer does not tell you whether the rest of the group has understood the idea.

4. Sort the responses into three useful groups

  • Secure: the learner gives a correct answer and can explain the key step.
  • Partly secure: the learner understands some of the process but misses a step or needs a prompt.
  • Needs another explanation: the response shows a misconception or the learner cannot yet begin independently.

Use these groups as planning notes, not labels for the learner. One short response is only a snapshot. Compare it with the learner's earlier work and questions during the lesson.

5. Turn the evidence into the next teaching step

If the answer is secure, give a new question with a slightly different context. If the learner is partly secure, revisit the missing step with a simpler example. If the learner has a misconception, explain the underlying idea again using a visual model, number line or concrete example before increasing the difficulty.

Give feedback that tells the learner what was done correctly and what to try next. The Education Endowment Foundation's guidance on feedback emphasises setting clear learning intentions and using formative assessment to identify learning gaps before feedback is given.

A five-minute routine

  • Minute 1: explain the three prompts and the learning goal.
  • Minutes 2–3: let the learner answer independently.
  • Minute 4: review the answers and ask one follow-up question.
  • Minute 5: agree on one next step for the next session.

The timing is a guide, not a rule. Give younger learners or students working on a new concept more time where needed.

EduMatch tutor note

Ask a question that reveals thinking, collect an answer from every learner, and decide what to do with the evidence. The exit ticket becomes useful when it changes the next teaching step.
